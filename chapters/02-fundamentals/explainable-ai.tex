\section{Explainable Artificial Intelligence}\label{sec:fund_xai}

As machine learning models increasingly support consequential decisions,
understanding \emph{why} a model produced a given output becomes as
important as its accuracy. Explainable artificial intelligence (XAI)
studies how to make a model's behavior understandable to a
human~\cite{guidotti2018survey, arrieta2020xai}. The literature commonly
organizes explanation methods along two dimensions: \textbf{intrinsic}
(interpretable by design, e.g.\ a small rule set) versus \textbf{post-hoc}
(an explanation produced after the fact for an otherwise fixed
model)~\cite{guidotti2018survey}, and \textbf{local} (one prediction)
versus \textbf{global} (the model's behavior overall)~\cite{arrieta2020xai}.
Figure~\ref{fig:xai_taxonomy} summarizes this orientation with
representative methods per quadrant.

Producing an explanation is not sufficient on its own: it must also be
evaluated. \textbf{Faithfulness} describes the degree to which an
explanation accurately reflects the reasoning the model actually used,
rather than a plausible-sounding story constructed after the
fact~\cite{jacovi2020faithfulness}. For explanations that consist of
cited evidence, such as graph edges, faithfulness can be tested by
intervention: an explanation is \emph{necessary} if removing the cited
evidence changes the prediction, and \emph{sufficient} if the cited
evidence alone still reproduces it. A related property,
\textbf{sparsity}, concerns how concise an explanation is: fewer, more
decisive pieces of evidence are easier to check than an exhaustive list.
SHAP~\cite{lundberg2017shap} is a widely used post-hoc, local method that
attributes a prediction to input features via a game-theoretic notion of
contribution.

This taxonomy directly informs SQ2 (Section~\ref{sec:intro_rq}), which
asks how faithfully each of the three model families explains its own
predictions. The rule-based baseline is intrinsic and local, since its
prediction is the matching rule itself; knowledge graph embeddings and
the relational graph neural network are both post-hoc and local,
explained respectively through the training facts and the
message-passing neighborhood (via
GNNExplainer~\cite{ying2019gnnexplainer}) that most influenced a
prediction. Converting all three into a common representation -- a set
of cited graph edges -- is what allows the necessary-and-sufficient
intervention test above to be applied uniformly: Chapter~\ref{ch:setup}
defines this shared protocol and applies it to the predictions evaluated
in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}.
