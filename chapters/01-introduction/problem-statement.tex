\section{Problem Statement and Research Questions}\label{sec:intro_rq}

The preceding motivation established why reliable causer attribution
matters, both for BMW's quality management process and, more generally,
for industrial decision-making under uncertainty. Building on this
motivation, this section formalizes the resulting problem and derives the
research questions that guide the remainder of this thesis.

Causer attribution at BMW is decided at the first call, from a sparse defect
description, and confirmed only later through the supplier's acknowledgement
in the 8D process or through internal assignment. The current exact-match
search supports this decision only when an identical historical case exists,
and it ignores partially matching cases and rework patterns. Learned models
can use such partial evidence, but their usefulness depends on whether the
evidence they present actually reflects the basis of their predictions.

This thesis formulates first-call causer attribution as semi-inductive link
prediction on an event-centric knowledge graph: given a new incident node
connected only to the information available at the time of the first
decision, predict its missing \texttt{CAUSED\_BY} link to a candidate causer.
In this thesis, \texttt{CAUSED\_BY} denotes the organizational responsibility
recorded after confirmation, not a physical cause-and-effect mechanism. The
following research questions guide the work.

\paragraph{Main research question.}
\emph{How accurately can the causer of a newly detected quality incident be
predicted at the time of the first causer decision by link prediction on an
event-centric knowledge graph, and how faithfully do the explanations of
symbolic, tensor-factorization and message-passing models reflect the basis
of their predictions as incidents become less similar to historical
cases?}

\begin{description}[%
  style=nextline,
  leftmargin=0pt,
  labelsep=0pt,
  font=\normalfont\bfseries,
  itemsep=0.5\baselineskip,
  topsep=\smallskipamount
]
  \item[SQ1 -- Generalization]
    How does the predictive performance of a rule-based baseline, knowledge
    graph embeddings and a relational graph convolutional network change
    across novelty levels, from incidents with an exact historical match to
    incidents whose attribute combination has never been observed?

  \item[SQ2 -- Faithfulness]
    How faithful are the explanations produced by each model family,
    measured with a shared protocol that tests whether the cited evidence is
    necessary and sufficient for the prediction, and how does their
    faithfulness change across novelty levels?

  \item[SQ3 -- Validity]
    % TODO drop SQ3 and H3's synthetic part if the synthetic dataset is cut
    Do the patterns observed on the BMW knowledge graph hold on a synthetic
    event-centric graph with planted causer rules, in which the true
    explanation of each prediction is known?
\end{description}

The research questions are tested through three hypotheses:

\begin{description}[%
  style=nextline,
  leftmargin=0pt,
  labelsep=0pt,
  font=\normalfont\bfseries,
  itemsep=0.4\baselineskip,
  topsep=\smallskipamount
]
  \item[H1]
    Message-passing models outperform the rule-based baseline on partially
    and fully novel incidents, but not on incidents with an exact historical
    match.

  \item[H2]
    Explanation faithfulness decreases as model expressiveness increases,
    from rules to embeddings to message passing.

  \item[H3]
    The faithfulness gap between model families widens as the novelty of
    test incidents increases.
\end{description}
