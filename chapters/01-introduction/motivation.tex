When a defect is identified at an inspection point in production, the first
question is who caused it. At BMW Group, this question is answered within
minutes of detection by the Quality Management Technology (QMT) front desk.
The answer is usually given verbally first and then recorded as the initial
causer allocation. It decides everything that follows: a supplier-related
defect leads to a quality notification in Qcube (SAP), a supplier complaint
and the 8D problem-solving process, whereas an internal defect is passed to
assembly, logistics, parts quality or another internal unit for
investigation.

This first decision is confirmed only much later. For supplier cases, the
confirmation is the supplier's acknowledgement of responsibility, which is
recorded in the 8D process, typically during the problem-description stage
(D2). Until then, the organization works on the basis of the first decision.
If the supplier rejects responsibility, the 8D process was misdirected: time
has been spent on supplier communication, sorting and containment, a dispute
may follow, and the internal investigation starts late. Conversely, if a
genuine supplier defect is first classified as internal, supplier containment
is delayed while defective parts may continue to arrive. Both errors increase
lead time, rework and cost.

Today, the first decision relies on expert judgment and a structured
repeat-failure search. This search looks up historical incidents with the
same combination of supplier number, part number and standardized defect
description: defect type, defect place and defect location. It is
transparent and works well when an identical case has occurred before.
However, it returns nothing when no identical case exists, it gives no weight
to cases that differ in only one attribute, and it does not systematically
use the rework history. Yet the rework history carries useful evidence: the
type of rework activity performed on a defective part is informative about
its likely causer, and repeated rework on the same part, or on the same part
with the same defect, reveals whether a new incident is a repeat or part of
a series.

The aim of this thesis is to support the first decision with a prediction of
the causer that the later confirmation will establish, made only from the
information available at that moment, and to show the evidence behind it:
similar historical incidents, whether the new incident appears to be a repeat
or part of a series, and the containment actions and root causes recorded for
those historical cases. A reliable early prediction would allow internal
defects to be handled internally from the start, allow supplier defects to be
booked to the supplier before a long investigation, and flag likely rejections
before an 8D process is opened. The objective is to support the front desk,
not to replace its judgment.

\subsection{Scientific Motivation}\label{sec:intro_motivation_sci}

The situation described above is not specific to automotive quality
management. Many organizations receive a stream of new events, each
described by a small number of categorical attributes that link it to
entities shared with past events, and must decide early who is responsible,
while the correct answer is confirmed only later. Examples include the
routing of IT incident tickets to resolver teams, the attribution of
warranty claims to suppliers, and the assignment of maintenance and incident
reports to departments.

Represented as a knowledge graph, each event becomes a node connected to its
attribute entities, and predicting the responsible party becomes the
prediction of a missing link for a node that did not exist when the model
was trained. This is a semi-inductive link prediction task: the event itself
is new, but the entities it connects to are
known~\cite{ali2021hyperrelational}. Three broad families of models can
address it. Symbolic methods apply explicit rules, such as the current
exact-match search. Tensor-factorization models, or knowledge graph
embeddings, learn vector representations in which similar cases lie close
together~\cite{bordes2013transe, trouillon2016complex}. Message-passing
models, such as relational graph convolutional networks, compute a
representation of a node from its neighborhood in the
graph~\cite{schlichtkrull2018rgcn}. Earlier work has shown that rule-based
and embedding-based link prediction succeed on different kinds of
queries~\cite{meilicke2018finegrained}.

Two questions remain open for this setting. The first concerns novelty.
Standard evaluations report performance averaged over all test queries. In
an event-centric graph, however, the novelty of a test event can be graded
by how much of its attribute combination has been observed before, which
makes it possible to ask how each model family behaves as events move away
from known precedents. The second concerns faithfulness. Each model family
explains its predictions differently: a rule names the matching historical
cases, an embedding model can be explained by the training facts that
support its prediction, and a graph neural network by the subgraph that
influenced it. These explanations are different objects and are rarely
compared under the same measure. Yet an explanation is only useful if it
reflects the actual basis of the prediction~\cite{jacovi2020faithfulness}. A
plausible expectation is that explanations agree and remain faithful when an
exact precedent exists, but that more flexible models continue to predict on
novel events while their explanations no longer reflect what drove the
prediction. In a decision setting in which a person must check the evidence
before acting, this matters as much as accuracy.

This thesis therefore studies predictive performance and explanation
faithfulness jointly, across levels of novelty, for all three model
families. BMW's quality data provide the real-world case.
